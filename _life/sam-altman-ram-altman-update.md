*This is an update to [the October 31 post on Altman's original DRAM remarks](/life/altman-ram-data-centers/). The October 29 Cheeky Pint podcast quote and the unverified 5% figure are recapped only briefly below; the new developments here are all from November 4, 2026.*

## What happened on November 4

Six days after the podcast, the backlash had not cooled — so Sam Altman tried to own the joke. On November 4, he changed his X display name to "RAM Altman" and posted, "i want you to have so much ram" and "lets build more." In a follow-up post the same day, he went further, writing that he does "want the world to have tons of memory and people to put as much as they want in their computers," and that "it is fucking annoying that this was taken out of context," adding that he has "been arguing for the world to ramp up memory production for a long time."

That is the whole damage-control package in one move: concede nothing on the substance, reframe the quote as a misreading, and wrap it in a meme. Video Games Chronicle reported the "taken out of context" post on November 4; AndroidHeadlines noted that Altman had already restored his usual name by the time its story was written. The rename lasted about as long as a news cycle.

## Why the joke did not land

The problem with the reframe is that the original quote was not actually ambiguous. On the October 29 Cheeky Pint podcast, hosted by Stripe co-founder John Collison, Altman said it was "quite terrible," from a global allocation perspective, "to have everybody have a bunch of the world's limited supply of DRAM sitting in their own computer and being used 5% of the time," and that "you want maximum utilization in the cloud if you're trying to deal with this very supply-constrained environment." Nobody had to take that out of context to be annoyed by it: the context *is* the annoyance. The argument is that a scarce resource should flow to data centers first, and personal computers are a low-priority use of it.

Elon Musk joined the pile-on with the line that wrote itself: "The people deserve to have their RAM too!" Gamers and PC builders, who have watched memory prices climb all year, treated the rename as confirmation rather than contrition — Altman heard the criticism, understood it perfectly, and still thinks the allocation logic is right.

To be fair to Altman, one part of his November 4 defense is true and important: he did not propose taking anyone's RAM away, banning consumer memory, or capping what people can buy. The 5% figure remains his characterization, not a measured statistic — no dataset behind it was cited in the interview — and the original post here said so on October 31. His call to "build more" memory is also the one fix everyone agrees on. But "build more" is a years-long fab story, and it does nothing for someone pricing a PC build this week.

## The market Altman is arguing about

The reason this keeps detonating is that the shortage is not theoretical. TrendForce projected conventional DRAM contract prices would rise 10–15% quarter over quarter in Q4 2026 as suppliers prioritize higher-value server memory, and SK Hynix has been reported as effectively sold out of DRAM and high-bandwidth memory through 2029. The same week, Microsoft announced Windows 11 tuning aimed at making 8 GB PCs last longer — the consumer side of the industry quietly admitting that the RAM buyers used to take for granted is now a constraint to design around.

That is the real split in this fight. One side treats memory as a shared utility to be allocated to its highest-utilization user. The other treats a PC's memory as something already bought, already owned, and already doing jobs — games, creative work, offline tasks — that a data-center round trip cannot replace. Altman's November 4 posts defend the first view while insisting he also believes the second. Both can be true in the abstract. In a shortage, they cannot both win the next wafer, and everyone in the thread knows which side OpenAI is bidding on.

## The judgment

Damage control works when it changes the story. "RAM Altman" changed the headline for a day and left the argument exactly where it was: the CEO of one of the world's biggest new DRAM buyers telling consumers that their share of a scarce resource is the inefficiency. The rename was funny, the frustration was genuine, and the combination is why the story survived a second news cycle. Until supply actually goes "way up" — Altman's own phrase from the original interview — every attempt to reframe this will read the same way: the beneficiary of the shortage explaining why the shortage is everyone else's fault.
