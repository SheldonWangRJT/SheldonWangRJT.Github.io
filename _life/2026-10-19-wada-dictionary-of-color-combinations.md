## The book in one paragraph

*A Dictionary of Color Combinations* （配色事典） is a small, fat, pocket-sized book by the Japanese artist **Sanzo Wada (1883–1967)**. It contains **348 color combinations** and almost nothing else: no essays, no color wheel, no rules. Each page simply places two, three, or four colors next to each other, as if to say — *this works; look*. It was compiled from Wada's six-volume *Haishoku Sōkan* （配色総鑑）, published in 1933–34, and reissued by Seigensha in 2010. Germany's *Die Zeit* praised it as a source of inspiration full of "hues we didn't even know existed." Designers, art directors, fashion people, and interior stylists around the world keep a copy within arm's reach. After a friend asked me about it recently, I realized it deserves a permanent page here — both as an introduction for friends, and as a reference I can open whenever I need to pick colors and pick them well.

## The man: a painter who treated color as infrastructure

Wada Sanzo was not a guru with a system to sell. He was a working painter and designer who spent sixty years making color a discipline in Japan.

- Born in Hyōgo in 1883, he studied Western painting under Kuroda Seiki, graduated from the Tokyo School of Fine Arts, and spent 1907–1915 in Europe.
- In **1927** he founded the Japan Standard Color Association — the ancestor of today's Japan Color Research Institute — because he believed color choices should be studied systematically, not left to taste alone.
- In **1932** he became a professor of design at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts. In 1936 he designed the poster for the 1940 Tokyo Olympics — the Games that were later cancelled.
- In **1951** he completed Japan's first comprehensive standard color chart, *Color Standards*.
- In **1953** he handled color and costume design for Daiei's film *Gate of Hell* （地獄門）. The film won the Palme d'Or at Cannes, and Wada won the **Academy Award for Best Costume Design** — the colors were the point of the film, and everyone knew it.
- In **1958** the Japanese government named him a Person of Cultural Merit.

So when this man published color combinations in 1933, he was not guessing. He was publishing the working notes of the person who literally set Japan's color standards.

## Where the book came from

Between 1933 and 1934, Wada published the *Haishoku Sōkan* in six volumes, documenting **over a thousand color combinations**. The original audience was practical: kimono makers, fashion and textile people, printers — anyone whose living depended on colors that harmonize. Japan in the early Shōwa era was absorbing Western dress, Western printing, and Western advertising all at once, and Wada wanted to record a distinctly Japanese perception of color — subtle, muted, nature-derived — before it dissolved.

The Seigensha edition distills that work into **348 combinations built from 159 colors**: 120 two-color, 120 three-color, and 108 four-color palettes. Every color carries a name and a number; the back of the book has a CMYK chart for every color and perforated swatch chips you can tear out and rearrange into combinations of your own. Text is in Japanese and English.

## Volume 2: the seasons and the streets

The sequel, *A Dictionary of Color Combinations Vol. 2* (Seigensha, 2020), revives two more of Wada's works from the 1930s:

- **72 seasonal combinations** themed on the twelve months of the Japanese year, three to seven colors each — plum-blossom months, rainy months, festival months, each with its own weather of color.
- **165 applied examples** drawn from 1910s–1930s fashion, interior, and graphic design — what people actually wore, lived in, and printed in Taishō and early Shōwa Japan.
- Color names in Japanese and English, including traditional Japanese color names such as 長春色 (chōshun-iro, a spring plum pink), 花淺葱 (hana-asagi, a light indigo blue), and 梅鼠 (ume-nezu, a plum-gray). The names are half the pleasure: colors named after plants, minerals, weather, and food remember where they came from.
- 172 removable color chips.

![A Dictionary of Color Combinations, Vol. 2 (Seigensha, 2020)](/assets/images/life/2026-10-19-wada-color-dictionary/cover-vol2.jpg)

If Vol. 1 is the grammar, Vol. 2 is the literature: combinations in the wild, attached to months, clothes, rooms, and posters.

## Why it works (and why most color advice doesn't)

Most color guidance gives you rules — complementary, analogous, triadic — and leaves you alone with a color wheel to execute them. Wada gives you **verdicts instead of rules**. A lifetime of looking, compressed into *these go together*. Three things make his verdicts age so well:

1. **Harmony over contrast.** Most palettes share an undertone, a temperature, a weather. Even the loud ones never shout.
2. **Muted bases.** Wada avoided saturated primaries. His red leans terracotta, his blue leans slate, his yellow leans ochre. Muted colors are forgiving: they survive bad lighting, cheap printing, and changing taste.
3. **Names from the physical world.** A color called "Burnt Sienna" or "Sudan Brown" tells you how to feel about it before you see it. The vocabulary is a design tool in itself.

The honest limitation: the book gives combinations, not proportions. It won't tell you how much of each color to use. My working rule, stolen from how the palettes look when they're printed as equal blocks and then applied: let one color dominate, one support, and treat the rest as accents — roughly 60 / 30 / 10 for three colors. Wada supplies the cast; you still have to direct.

### Postscript: this website

This site's current design — ink black, paper white, one restrained gold — was chosen in October 2026 after I went back to this book and its way of thinking: few colors, clear jobs, no decoration doing the work that color should do.

## How to get it, and how to browse it free

- **Vol. 1** — Seigensha, 2010. ISBN 978-4-86152-247-5. **Vol. 2** — Seigensha, 2020. ISBN 978-4-86152-772-2.
- Both are widely stocked by art bookstores and online retailers. When I checked on **October 19, 2026**, Galen Leather listed both volumes at **$28 each** — prices vary by retailer and over time, so treat that as a snapshot, not a quote.
- Free to browse: an interactive version of all 348 combinations lives at [sanzo-wada.dmbk.io](https://sanzo-wada.dmbk.io), and [Wada Sanzo Colors](https://www.wada-sanzo-colors.com/) offers HEX/RGB/LAB values, a color picker that finds the closest Wada color to any color you give it, and iOS/Android apps. The physical book is still the better object — the printed swatches are the real colors, and the tear-out chips are genuinely useful — but the sites are perfect for a quick decision at your desk.

If you buy one design book this year and you make anything visual — slides, sites, rooms, outfits, gifts — make it this one. It is ninety years old, fits in a coat pocket, and has never once given me a palette that embarrassed me later.
