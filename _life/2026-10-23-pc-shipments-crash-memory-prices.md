*All shipment figures are Counterpoint Research preliminary estimates for Q3 2026, published October 22–23, 2026, as reported by The Verge, 9to5Mac, and MacRumors. Component contract prices are Counterpoint's. The smartphone figures are from Counterpoint's earlier Q3 release (October 12, via Reuters), included because it is the same crisis in the adjacent aisle. Forecasts are Counterpoint's, not facts.*

## The table

| Vendor | Q3 share | Shipments YoY |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lenovo | 26.3% | −23% |
| HP | 18.1% | −31% |
| Dell | 13.3% | −25% |
| Apple | 9.8% | −11% (5.6M units) |
| ASUS | 9.2% | −7% |
| **Total market** | **57M units** | **−21% YoY, −13.8% QoQ** |

Two details do the analytical work. First, the ranking of pain is a ranking of exposure to the low end: HP, the most consumer-and-budget-weighted of the big three, fell hardest; ASUS and Apple — one with a differentiated gaming/creator mix, one premium with a new entry model, the MacBook Neo, cushioning it — fell least and *gained* share while shrinking. Apple "grew" from 8.7% to 9.8% of a collapsing market by falling more slowly. That is what winning looks like in this phase of the cycle.

Second, the comparison base is flattering and it still looks like this. Q3 2025 was inflated by purchases pulled ahead of the Windows 10 support deadline and tariff-related inventory moves, per Counterpoint — so some of the −21% is arithmetic. But Counterpoint's own forward line removes the comfort: **Q4 2026 shipments are expected to fall 27%**, and 2027 is forecast "broadly flat." Associate director David Naranjo's phrasing deserves quoting because analysts rarely talk this way: "stabilization should not be mistaken for recovery."

## The mechanism: two contract prices

This is not a vibes downturn. Counterpoint put numbers on the input side: contract prices for **8GB DDR4 modules rose 24% quarter over quarter in Q3**, and **1TB NVMe SSDs rose 29%**. The forecast is for more of the same — RAM up another 13% in Q4 and 15% in Q1 2027, SSDs up 11% in Q4. The memory industry has one customer class bidding without a budget ceiling (AI data centers, on multi-year contracts) and everyone else bidding with a retail price tag they cannot move. The memory goes where the margin is; PCs, and especially cheap PCs, get what is left at the price that remains.

The industry response tells you how structural this is. Per The Verge's reporting of the Counterpoint release, vendors are **reviving older technology — DDR4 memory and 4GB GPUs — to mitigate the hikes**, and shifting mix toward "configurations that can better absorb component inflation," in senior analyst Minsoo Kang's words. Translate: the affordable end of the market is being abandoned on purpose, because at current component prices an honest cheap laptop is a loss-maker. The average machine gets more premium; the entry machine quietly leaves the lineup.

## The aisle next door already had this quarter

Smartphones ran the same experiment one release earlier. Counterpoint's Q3 smartphone numbers (October 12, via Reuters): global shipments **−20% year over year, the worst Q3 since 2012**, on the same memory-cost mechanism. Samsung held first place at 23% share with shipments down just 3% — it makes its own memory, which in 2026 turns out to be a consumer-electronics strategy. Apple took a record Q3 share of 22% while its own shipments also fell. And the low end was not squeezed, it was amputated: Xiaomi's shipments fell more than 30% as it cut exposure to cheaper devices, with OPPO and vivo down more than 20% each. Counterpoint's Shilpi Jain: consumers are holding devices longer and turning to refurbished ones.

## My call

We have spent two years asking whether the AI build-out would crowd out consumer hardware. Q3 2026 is the quarter the answer stopped being theoretical — the same week, notably, that this site wrote up leaked PS6 bills of materials with memory and storage at $680 of a ~$960 console. PCs, phones, and consoles are three readings of one ledger: a fixed pool of DRAM and NAND, reallocated by price to the buyer with the deepest pockets, which is no longer the person buying a laptop for school.

Two implications follow. First, "flat 2027" is the optimistic branch, and it still means the installed base ages another year — expect the refurbished market, not the new market, to be the growth story in consumer devices. Second, the vendors gaining share in a crash — Apple with a cheaper Neo in the mix, ASUS, Samsung with vertical integration — share one trait: control over either their supply or their price ladder. In a component famine, that is the whole game. The brands that merely assemble other people's memory into other people's price points are the ones printing −31%.
