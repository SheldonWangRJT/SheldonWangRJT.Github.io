*What Apple has confirmed: the event — media invites went out October 19, 2026 for a "Pro and tell" launch experience in Los Angeles on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, teased by marketing chief Greg Joswiak ("Can't wait to pro and tell next week"). Every product detail below is attributed reporting (Bloomberg's Mark Gurman, analyst Ming-Chi Kuo, supply-chain sources), not Apple statements, as of October 21, 2026.*

## The quote Apple has to outrun

On October 20, 2010, at the "Back to the Mac" event, Steve Jobs explained — in the past tense, as a settled matter — why there would never be a touchscreen Mac:

> "We've done tons of user testing on this, and it turns out it doesn't work. Touch surfaces don't want to be vertical. It gives great demo, but after a short period of time you start to fatigue, and after an extended period of time your arm wants to fall off. It doesn't work. It's ergonomically terrible. Touch surfaces want to be horizontal."

Apple then spent fifteen years re-litigating that sentence in public, always reaching the same verdict:

- **2012 — Tim Cook**, asked about Surface-style convergence: "You can converge a toaster and a refrigerator, but these things are probably not going to be pleasing to the user."
- **2015 — Cook again:** "We feel strongly that customers are not really looking for a converged Mac and iPad."
- **2016 — Jony Ive:** Apple had explored touchscreen Mac designs that "conceptually make sense" but, lived with daily, proved "less compelling."
- **2016 — Phil Schiller**, confirming years of internal prototypes: "We've absolutely come away with the belief that it isn't the right thing to do. Our instincts were correct."
- **2020 — Craig Federighi**, as macOS Big Sur's iOS-like redesign sparked touch speculation: Apple designed it "in a way that felt most comfortable and natural to us, **not remotely considering something about touch**."
- **2021 — John Ternus**, then head of hardware engineering: "We make the world's best touch computer on an iPad," and the Mac is "optimized for indirect input."

That last quote is the delicious one. John Ternus is now Apple's CEO — Reuters noted this month that he is pushing faster product cycles — and the touchscreen MacBook Pro is expected to headline *his* event next Tuesday. The executive who articulated the doctrine in 2021 is the one presiding over its funeral.

## What is reportedly launching

Per Gurman's Bloomberg reporting (corroborated by Kuo and supply-chain sources; Samsung Display reportedly began producing the OLED touch panels in January 2026):

- **14-inch and 16-inch MacBook Pro with Tandem OLED** — the stacked two-layer panel tech from the iPad Pro — with **on-cell touch** integrated into the display. First OLED MacBook, first touchscreen Mac.
- **The notch dies**: replaced by a hole-punch camera wrapped in a **Dynamic Island** that expands for alerts and Live Activities.
- A **reinforced hinge** so the screen doesn't wobble when tapped, and a machine that is **"significantly lighter"** after an internal redesign (Gurman has walked back "thinner" — expect lighter, not dramatically thinner).
- It sells **alongside** the current MacBook Pro line as a more expensive tier — reported price math ranges from a ~20% premium (Bloomberg's analogy to the iPad Pro's OLED jump; roughly $2,639 for a 14-inch by one outlet's arithmetic) to 9to5Mac's guess of a **$500–$700 premium**. Nobody actually knows the price yet.
- **The chip twist**: the touchscreen flagships reportedly run the **existing M5 Pro and M5 Max** — because no M6 Pro/Max exists. The new 2nm **M6** (which debuted in the Mac mini in August) goes into a conventional-design 14-inch MacBook Pro and an M6 iMac at the same event. Yes, that means the halo machine may ship with the older silicon generation while the cheaper model gets the newer chip. Several outlets have this backwards; the latest Bloomberg reporting is clear about it.
- **On sale Friday, November 6**, per reports, with Apple Stores closing the evening of November 5 to reset floors.
- Also expected October 28: an **iPad mini 8 with OLED**, its FaceTime camera moved to the landscape edge, and a revamped speaker system.

The software is half the product. Gurman reports Apple is testing the machines on **macOS 27.2** under a philosophy of **"touch-friendly, not touch-first"**: tap a menu-bar item and the surrounding controls enlarge; contextual menus appear near your finger; sliders, buttons, and Control Center elements have been reworked to be finger-usable; new touch gestures are appearing in Photos, Notes, and Safari betas. The keyboard and trackpad remain the primary interface — the screen is explicitly *not* designed for typing on. In retrospect, macOS Tahoe's Liquid Glass redesign — all that extra space around icons and sliders — was the tell.

## Why now: four forces, one confession

**The users changed.** Kuo's September 2025 supply-chain note gave the analyst version: the shift "appears to reflect Apple's long-term observation of iPad user behavior, indicating that in certain scenarios, touch controls can enhance both productivity and the overall user experience." Translated: fifteen years of iPhone and iPad have trained hundreds of millions of people to poke screens, including laptop screens. Jobs' 2010 test subjects had owned a multitouch device for about three years; today's Mac buyers were raised on them.

**The checklist changed.** Touch is standard on premium Windows laptops from Dell, Lenovo, Samsung, and Microsoft's own Surface line. Apple was the last holdout, and "no touchscreen" had become a line item lost in spec-sheet comparisons rather than a philosophy admired.

**The doctrine had already collapsed from the other side.** Apple sells the iPad Pro with a Magic Keyboard — a touchscreen with a trackpad in a laptop form factor, running an increasingly Mac-like OS. The company insisting "touch computers and laptops are different categories" has been selling their convergence for years; the touchscreen MacBook Pro is less a reversal than an admission of where the border already was.

**The business changed.** Mac just posted record revenue, and with yearly chip gains getting harder for buyers to *feel*, an OLED + touch + lighter redesign is visible differentiation that can defend premium pricing. The Street called it what it is: "Apple's touchscreen MacBook is really a pricing test."

## The skeptics are not convinced — and one poll stings

9to5Mac's Ben Lovejoy spoke for the shrug majority this week: the excitement is about OLED; the touchscreen "will have limited utility and be a fingerprint magnet." His own iPad Pro experience: "I used the trackpad for everything except scrolling." His pricing poll is the sharpest framing of the problem — at a guessed $500–$700 premium, the alternative purchase is **doubling your RAM or SSD** in the existing MacBook Pro. Which is the better deal? Most pro users know their answer.

The gorilla-arm critique also hasn't expired: human arms and vertical screens are the same as in 2010, and Apple's own mitigation — touch as occasional supplement, hinge reinforced — is unproven until hands-ons land October 28. And there is the lineup tightrope The Street flagged: make the MacBook Pro too iPad-like and you invite the question of why both products exist, which may be why Apple reportedly won't market this as a convertible at all.

## The honest reading: Apple isn't saying Jobs was wrong

Read the reported design carefully and it concedes Jobs' actual point. He objected to vertical touch as a *primary, sustained* input — the demo that delights for a minute and fatigues for an hour. The 2026 machine keeps keyboard and trackpad primary, adds touch for the moments it genuinely wins (scrolling, zooming, tapping a target that's already under your eye, the Dynamic Island's glanceable layer), and engineers the hinge against the wobble that made earlier attempts feel cheap. Federighi's line — touch Macs were "not remotely" considered — was true of a Mac that replaced the trackpad with a touchscreen. This is not that Mac.

Whether "touch as supplement" is a principled distinction or a sixteen-year rationalization is now an empirical question, and it gets answered in about a week: hands-on reports October 28, machines in stores November 6, and a price tag that tells us whether Apple thinks touch is a feature — or a tier. If the premium really lands at $500–700, the market will deliver the verdict Jobs predicted or the one Ternus is betting on, one purchase at a time.

*Context: the touchscreen Pros are the third beat of an unusually dense Apple fall — iPhone Duo on sale October 23, HomePod mini 2 October 30, these Macs and the iPad mini November 6, HomeView and the new Apple TV 4K November 13.*
