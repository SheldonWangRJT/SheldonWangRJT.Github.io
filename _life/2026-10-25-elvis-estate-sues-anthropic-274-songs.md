*Everything in the allegations sections is the plaintiffs' claim, as filed October 23, 2026 (ABE EPE LLC v. Anthropic) and reported by Billboard, Rolling Stone, NME, Parade and Far Out on October 24–25. No court has ruled on any of it. Anthropic had not publicly responded as of October 25; its prior position in similar cases — that training can be protected fair use — is noted where relevant.*

## The filing

On **Friday, October 23, 2026**, a subsidiary of **Authentic Brands Group** — the licensing group that controls much of Elvis Presley's catalogue, trademarks, name and likeness — sued **Anthropic** alongside other Presley rights holders. The complaint alleges that Anthropic trained its Claude models on **274 Elvis songs** without permission or payment, naming hits including "Can't Help Falling in Love," "All Shook Up," "Don't Be Cruel," "Return to Sender" and "A Little Less Conversation."

The damages arithmetic is statutory and simple: **$150,000 per song × 274 songs ≈ $41.1 million**. Lead attorney **Richard Busch** framed it maximally: the plaintiffs sue a defendant that "has built its entire business model based on copyright infringement at a scale never seen before." Billboard described the case as the most prominent legal action so far brought **on behalf of a single musician** — earlier music suits against AI companies came from labels, publishers and industry bodies, not from one artist's estate.

Busch's name is itself an argument. He represented **Marvin Gaye's estate** in the *Blurred Lines* case, which ended with a ruling that Robin Thicke and Pharrell Williams's 2013 hit infringed Gaye's "Got to Give It Up" — a verdict that widened what copyright in music is understood to protect, and that working musicians still argue about.

## The quote the complaint is built around

The filing's sharpest exhibit is not a training-data log. It is Claude's own words. Per the complaint, as quoted by Rolling Stone, even after Anthropic "supposedly put up 'guardrails'" following earlier suits, Claude — asked to recreate specific songs "for a modern audience" — produced material that, in the model's own assessment:

> "These renders keep landing very close to the originals: same structure, same hooks reused with light rewording, and full sets of verses/choruses/bridges. That's edging past 'inspired by' into reproducing the copyrighted song, even when the words are swapped out."

Treat this carefully: it is a quote inside a plaintiff's complaint, selected by the plaintiffs, and a chatbot's self-description is not a technical measurement. But as litigation craft it is devastating, because it moves the argument from *training* (where Anthropic's fair-use defence is strongest and largely untested at this scale) to *output* — what the product does today, in public, after the company said it had fixed it. The complaint's broader claim follows the same route: that Claude "has created infringing output of copyrighted material of virtually every song from every catalog available on the internet."

## The queue Anthropic is already standing in

This is not Anthropic's first music case, and the timeline matters:

- **October 2023:** Universal Music Publishing Group, Concord and ABKCO sued Anthropic over Claude reproducing copyrighted lyrics (Katy Perry, Gloria Gaynor, the Rolling Stones).
- **August 2026:** Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell sued over lyrics and sheet music spanning the Beatles, Taylor Swift and Michael Jackson.
- **September 2026:** Jason Isbell and other musicians sued AI music generator **Suno** — a different defendant, the same theory of the case spreading to living artists acting for themselves.
- **October 23, 2026:** the Elvis estate's suit.

Two things changed across that sequence. The plaintiffs moved closer to the artist — publisher, then label-affiliated publishers, then individual musicians, now a single iconic estate. And the alleged wrong moved from reproduction of lyrics toward training-plus-output as one continuous act. Anthropic's standing answer — no comment on this filing as of October 24–25; in prior litigation, that training on copyrighted material can be fair use — has not yet been tested to a final judgment in any of these.

## Why $41 million is not the stake

Set the number against the plaintiff's target: $41.1 million is a rounding error for a company that, per draft S-1 reporting covered [in our October 22 post](/life/anthropic-ipo-80-billion-loss/), booked $16.3 billion of revenue in a half-year. Nobody files this case for the cheque.

The estate's real asset is position. An iconic dead artist cannot be cross-examined, cannot settle cheaply without headlines, and carries a catalogue whose value *is* its licensing discipline — Authentic Brands' whole business is charging for Elvis. If a court accepts that (a) training on the catalogue was infringing and (b) guardrails that fail make the *output* a continuing violation, the precedent prices every major catalogue in the world for every frontier lab — and converts a legal theory the industry has treated as background risk into a line item.

My judgment: the training claim faces the same fair-use wall as its predecessors, and may take years to resolve. The **output-and-guardrails claim is the dangerous one for Anthropic**, because it is checkable by anyone with a Claude account, it survives even a fair-use win on training, and the plaintiffs have helpfully quoted the product confessing to it. Expect Anthropic's response to fight hardest exactly there — and expect every other estate with a valuable dead catalogue to be watching October 23's filing as a template. Elvis left the building in 1977. As a plaintiff, he may just be arriving.
