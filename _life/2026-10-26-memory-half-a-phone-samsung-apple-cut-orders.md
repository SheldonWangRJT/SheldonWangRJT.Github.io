Samsung Display's revenue concentration is from its audited 2025 financial statements. Treat the cuts as reported, not announced.*

## Two reports, one morning, one cause

On **Monday, October 26, 2026**, South Korea's two main business outlets published matching accounts of the same retreat:

**The Korea Herald** reported that **Samsung Electronics' mobile division has asked suppliers to reduce fourth-quarter component deliveries by 20–30%** versus earlier plans, and that **Apple has cut October orders for certain iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max components by 15–20%** — during the flagship launch window, when orders for high-end OLED panels normally rise. The Herald noted Samsung usually trims production ahead of a Galaxy S launch, but called these cuts "steeper than expected."

**Electronic Times (ETNews)**, citing supply-chain sources briefed October 25, narrowed the Samsung cut: **fourth-quarter production of the Galaxy A series is set about 20% below the plan made earlier this year — roughly 10 million fewer phones on a full-year view**, with a further cut of more than 10% under consideration for next year. Galaxy A is not a side line: it accounts for about **70% of Samsung's 230–240 million annual smartphone shipments**.

The stated cause in both reports is identical: **memory prices**. ETNews reports that on some midrange and budget phones, memory semiconductors now make up **more than half of total production cost**. Asia Daily's summary of the same reporting puts a number on the violence: an **8GB DRAM + 256GB NAND combination that cost $35 in Q3 2025 costs $140 in Q3 2026** — a 4× increase in twelve months.

## The circular firing squad inside Samsung

No company embodies this crisis like Samsung, because Samsung sits on every side of it at once:

- Its **semiconductor division** is posting record profits selling DRAM and NAND into the AI data-centre build-out — the demand that quadrupled that $35 combo.
- Its **MX (mobile) division** buys memory at those prices, has reportedly swung to losses this year, and is now cutting the volume line that keeps Samsung No. 1 in global units.
- Its **display division** sells the screens for both Samsung phones and iPhones — and gets squeezed from both directions.

That third leg is where October 26 gets dangerous. Samsung Display's audited 2025 accounts show two customer groups — widely understood to be Apple and Samsung Electronics — generated **40.4% and 22.4% of revenue respectively: 62.8% combined, ₩18.66 trillion (about $14.1 billion)**. When both customers cut orders in the same quarter, the damage is worse than the volume math: as the Herald's industry source put it, weaker factory utilisation raises the fixed cost of every panel, and losing *premium* iPhone panels degrades the sales mix — the question is not how many fewer panels, but which ones.

ETNews adds the strategic consequence: Samsung is redirecting scarce components toward higher-margin **Galaxy S and Galaxy Z** flagships. And the global ranking is suddenly in play — Counterpoint estimated in August that Samsung would finish 2026 first worldwide, ahead of Apple by **0.1 percentage point**. A 20% cut to the line that is 70% of your volume can erase a 0.1-point lead by itself. The background market is already brutal: IDC's Q3 tally put global shipments at **255.3 million units, down 22%** (Counterpoint's Q3 read: down 20%, the weakest Q3 since 2012).

## Why this is the end of something, not a bad quarter

This site has tracked chipflation category by category: console prices at record average tickets (September's Circana data), PC shipments crashing, the RTX 5090 effectively vanishing from retail. Phones were the holdout because phones had scale, long-term memory contracts, and — in Samsung's case — their own fabs. October 26 is the holdout falling, and it falls in a specific order that should worry everyone below the flagship line:

1. **Midrange dies first.** A flagship can pass a $100 memory bill to a buyer who finances over 36 months; a $250 Galaxy A cannot pass a bill that is now half its production cost. ETNews' reporting that Samsung is weighing *weaker displays and cameras at higher prices* for future models is the sound of the value segment being hollowed out.
2. **Volume is being sacrificed deliberately.** Both Samsung reports describe a choice: fewer, richer phones over more, cheaper ones. That protects margin per unit and abandons the installed-base race — the same trade the console industry made, arriving at the same destination.
3. **The supplier of the crisis is also its victim — and still wins.** Samsung the group nets out ahead: memory profits are group profits too. The pain is distributed to phone buyers, component suppliers, and Samsung Display's P&L. The AI build-out does not just compete with consumer hardware for chips; on October 26 it started eating the consumer business of the very company selling it the chips.

The number to remember is not the 20% cut. It is **$35 → $140**. Until that combo price bends back down, every category of consumer hardware — consoles in September, PCs, and now the phone in your pocket — is paying rent to the data centre.
