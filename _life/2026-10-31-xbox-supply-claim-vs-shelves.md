*This post follows up this site's September Circana coverage with a new event: Xbox CEO Asha Sharma's October 28 claim about console supply, and the rebuttal it drew on October 29–30. September figures appear only as context.*

## The claim

On October 28, 2026, alongside Microsoft's fiscal Q1 2027 results for the quarter ending September 30, Xbox CEO Asha Sharma posted on X that "console sales remain constrained by supply, with more demand than consoles available." The surrounding numbers were mixed: overall Xbox revenue declined **2% year over year** (an improvement on the prior quarter's 10% decline), hardware revenue fell **14%**, and Microsoft attributed that hardware decline to a lower volume of consoles sold. Sharma's bright spots were elsewhere: record performances from Minecraft and World of Warcraft: Forever, a Call of Duty release she described as its most acclaimed in more than a decade, and **double-digit growth in PC platform revenue**. Her proposed remedy for the console side was telling: "We're working to get more consoles into players' hands, including through our expanded refurbishment program."

Note what the post did not say. Sharma did not specify which markets or regions face shortages, or which channels. That omission matters, because the rebuttal arrived within a day and it was specific.

## The shelf test

GameSpot's October 29 report put it plainly: in the United States, you can buy an Xbox Series X|S at Best Buy, Target, Walmart, and on Xbox's own website. Circana senior director Mat Piscatella, the analyst whose retail tracking is the closest thing this industry has to a scoreboard, said on Bluesky that "the data I've seen do not suggest that stock has been the biggest driver of Xbox hardware performance." Xbox Series X|S hardware sales in the US keep setting record lows, and that comes on top of Xbox raising console prices again in August — a price increase makes a 14% hardware revenue decline harder, not easier, to explain as a pure shortage. A product in short supply does not usually get more expensive and sell fewer units at the same time unless something else is going on.

The September context sharpens the point rather than softening it. Circana's September data had Xbox recording its worst September for console unit sales on record, while PlayStation 5 led the month in both units and dollars on the back of Marvel's Wolverine. This site covered that split on October 24. Sharma's claim asks readers to believe that a multi-quarter slide, visible in the same dataset month after month, is principally a stock problem. Piscatella's answer is that the dataset says otherwise.

And on October 30, the critique widened from one claim to the whole turnaround. Speaking on The Game Business Show, Piscatella said that across the sales and engagement data he tracks, Sharma's initiatives so far are "not having any positive impact" — the disc-to-digital program has not changed the physical software trend, Gears exclusivity announcements have not moved the hardware trend, and Xbox has lost distribution in the US. He added one caveat that matters for fairness: it is too early to judge whether removing Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 from Game Pass at launch pushed more players to buy it outright. The hardware judgment, however, he stated without qualification: "the hardware trend keeps getting worse."

## What is actually true here

The careful version of this story allows for two things to be true at once. Supply can genuinely be tight in some region, some configuration, or some channel — Sharma's June predecessor in this argument, chief strategy officer Matthew Ball, said Xbox was "producing them as quickly as possible" with a "severe limitation to how quickly we can do that." Memory and component costs are rising across the industry, and that constraint is real for every console maker. But a global or general supply claim cannot survive contact with the US evidence: available stock at every major retailer, record-low sales, and a price hike in the same quarter. If demand exceeded supply in the United States, the shelves would be the first place to show it, and they show the opposite.

There is also a simpler explanation that fits all the numbers without special pleading: Xbox's center of gravity has moved. Sharma's own post is the evidence. The records are in Minecraft and World of Warcraft, the growth is on PC, the acclaimed release is a multi-platform franchise, and the console remedy on offer is refurbished units. That is a publishing and PC business describing itself in console language. The box is no longer the growth engine, and a supply story lets everyone avoid saying so out loud.

## The judgment — and what to do about it

For players, the practical conclusion is the opposite of what a shortage claim implies. Do not wait, do not panic-buy, and do not pay a premium for a box that is sitting on shelves. If you want an Xbox console, buy it on price and value — the August price increase means waiting for a promotion is rational in a way it would not be during a real shortage. If you mostly care about Xbox's games, Sharma's own highlights point the other way: PC, where the division says revenue is growing double digits, is where the momentum is, and Game Pass's changing terms (including Call of Duty no longer launching into it) deserve a harder look than the console's availability does.

For Xbox, the credibility cost of this claim is larger than the claim itself. Every quarter the company describes a demand problem as a supply problem, it spends trust it will need when it eventually has a real supply story to tell — for example, around the next-generation handheld reportedly targeted for holiday 2027 or the Helix console discussed for 2028, both of which will face the same memory-cost pressures. Piscatella's line is the one to remember, because it is falsifiable in a way Sharma's is not: stock has either been the biggest driver or it has not, and the data he tracks says it has not. Until Xbox names the regions and channels where consoles are actually unavailable, the shelf is the more credible witness.
