*All sales figures are Circana's US estimates for September 2026 (tracking period August 30–October 3), published by analyst Mat Piscatella on October 22, 2026, as reported by GamesRadar+, GameSpot, GamingBolt, and others cited below. US only — not global. Average prices are prices actually paid, not list prices. This post follows our October 21 analysis of leaked next-generation console costs — that was the supply side; this is the demand side answering.*

## The scoreboard

| September 2026 (US, Circana) | PS5 | Switch 2 | Xbox Series X\|S |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unit sales, year over year | **+22%** | −14% | **−27%** |
| Dollar sales, year over year | **+43%** | −12% | −2% |
| Average price paid | $646 (+17%) | $498 (+2%) | $663 (+33%) |
| Rank (units and dollars) | 1st | 2nd | 3rd |

Three facts in that table deserve emphasis:

1. **Xbox's −27% is a record, in the wrong direction.** Circana's Mat Piscatella confirmed Xbox ecosystem console units hit an **all-time low for a September** — breaking the previous record low, which was set in **September 2025**. Two consecutive record-low Septembers is not a bad month; it is a trajectory. Year-to-date through August, US Xbox hardware units were already down **33%**.
2. **PS5 did not merely win; it expanded.** Units +22% and dollars +43% gave Sony its **largest monthly share of US hardware dollars since May 2025** — the month before Switch 2 launched. The gap between the unit and dollar growth is the price increase compounding, willingly paid.
3. **The market shrank in boxes and grew in money.** Total hardware units fell **3%**, yet total hardware spending **rose 12% to $362 million**. Total US video-game spending (hardware, software, accessories) was about **$4.8 billion, down 1%**. Fewer consoles, at much higher prices, is now the industry's normal month — August 2026 was already the weakest August for unit volume since 2013, at a record average console price of $541.

On software, the month had a clear engine: **Marvel's Wolverine** (Insomniac, PS5) debuted at No. 1 and posted the **highest launch-month dollar sales for a Sony-published game since *Helldivers II* in February 2024**, landing at No. 11 year-to-date in under three weeks of tracking. *NBA 2K27* and *EA Sports FC 27* followed. Sony's hardware surge and Sony's software hit are the same event viewed from two sides.

## Three strategies for one price shock

The component crisis underneath these prices — AI data centers bidding up the DRAM and NAND that consoles are made of — is the same force behind the leaked PS6/Xbox Helix cost blowout we covered on October 21. September shows how differently the three incumbents are passing that shock to households:

**Sony: charge more, give them a reason.** The average PS5 transaction rose from **$551 to $646 (+17%)** — and units rose anyway. A console cycle normally cuts prices in year six; this one raised them and grew, because the catalogue moment (Wolverine now, the industry's November gravity still ahead) gave buyers a reason to pay the new price today rather than wait.

**Nintendo: hide the hike in the box.** Switch 2's US list price rose **$50, from $449.99 to $499.99 on September 1** — an 11% sticker increase that somehow produced only a **+2%** rise in average price paid ($486 → $498). Piscatella's explanation, praised by him as smart: the mix shifted away from the **$499 Mario Kart World bundle** toward the **$499.99 standalone console**. The household pays the same number at the register and takes home less — the game left the box. The hike is real; it is just denominated in content instead of dollars. Even so, Switch 2 units fell **14%** against a September 2025 that sat three months after its record launch — a tough comparison, and still a decline.

**Microsoft: charge the most, sell the fewest.** The average Xbox transaction is now the highest of the three at **$663, up 33% from $497** a year ago. That single number explains the strangest line in the table: units down 27% but dollars down only 2%. Microsoft did not hold its hardware business steady in September; it **held the revenue line by pricing the remaining buyers harder**. Piscatella has been explicit that higher hardware prices driven by the RAM and component crisis have significantly impacted selling rates for both PS5 and Xbox — but only one of them had a Wolverine to offset it.

## My call

September is the demand-side verdict on the argument from our console-crisis piece: the $499 console contract is over, and what replaces it is not a single new price but a **sorting mechanism**. At $646 average, PlayStation is proving a premium box survives if — and only if — it keeps delivering system-selling software on schedule. At $498 with the game removed from the box, Nintendo is proving the most disciplined version of the same trick: protect the headline number at all costs, because its buyers are families, not enthusiasts. At $663 with no equivalent exclusive pulling in the other direction, Xbox is proving the negative case: price without pull does not produce premium revenue for long, it produces record lows — twice in a row.

The number to watch into the holidays is not Xbox's unit rank, which is settled at third. It is whether that **$663 average holds while units keep falling**. A platform can live for a while on fewer, richer buyers. It cannot build the installed base that makes developers prioritize it — and an installed-base spiral, unlike a bad September, does not reset in October.
