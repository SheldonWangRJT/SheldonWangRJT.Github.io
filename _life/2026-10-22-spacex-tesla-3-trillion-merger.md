## What actually happened this week

Two earnings calls, forty-eight hours, one story.

On **October 20**, on SpaceX's quarterly earnings call, Elon Musk confirmed that **special committees of independent directors at both SpaceX and Tesla are now exploring a combination** of the two companies. He said he thinks it would be a good idea, that there is a lot of merit to combining them — and that he cannot take part in the talks, because as CEO of both companies he is a conflicted party. He called it, in his own words, an "odd kabuki dance."

On **October 21**, Tesla reported its third quarter — and took **no merger questions at all**. Investor relations said Musk had said everything that should be said the day before. A banned question on a public earnings call is its own kind of confirmation: this is no longer a rumor to be waved away, it is a live corporate process to be managed.

Markets voted immediately. SpaceX shares rose about **8% in extended trading** on the announcement (and as much as 10% premarket in some sessions); Tesla rose roughly 2%. Prediction markets, for what they are worth, put the odds of a deal at about **18.5% by the end of 2026 and 64% by the end of 2027** — speculation, not evidence, but a useful gauge of how seriously the idea is now taken.

## The numbers that make the pitch

The timing was not accidental. Musk floated the review on the same call where SpaceX posted the loudest quarter in its history:

- **SpaceX Q3 2026 revenue: $14.6 billion, up 161% year over year**, with net income of **$4.4 billion** — against a $1.1 billion loss a year ago.
- The standout: SpaceX's AI segment (Super Intelligence) generated **$7.56 billion** in the quarter, up roughly **830%** — and **overtook Starlink as the company's largest business for the first time**. Starlink itself is at about 13.6 million consumer subscribers.
- The combined valuation being discussed is roughly **$3.5 to $3.6 trillion** — Tesla at about $1.5 trillion, SpaceX at about $2 trillion after its June IPO. At current prices the combined group would employ about 155,000 people and generate roughly $153 billion in 2026 revenue, per analyst estimates compiled by Visible Alpha.

And Tesla's own quarter explains why the pitch lands now, from the other side:

- **Revenue $28.05 billion**, roughly flat year over year; **adjusted EPS $0.48**, a beat against roughly $0.43–0.45 expected.
- But **GAAP net income fell 43% to $787 million** and operating income fell 56%, as AI and Optimus spending surged and regulatory-credit revenue shrank.
- The bright spots are all future businesses: **FSD subscriptions up 67% to 1.74 million active**, **Optimus production has started in Fremont** with external deliveries targeted around the end of 2027, the Austin robotaxi fleet has grown 10x since launch — and meaningful robotaxi revenue is now a **2027** story, by management's own framing.
- Tesla also **cut its 2026 capex guide from "more than $25 billion" to about $20 billion**, which flattered free cash flow ($1.53 billion, positive) while quietly admitting the build-out is slower than promised.

Read the two reports together and the merger logic writes itself. Tesla is a car company whose valuation rests on AI, robots, and chips it does not yet sell at scale. SpaceX is suddenly an AI company with rockets attached — its AI segment alone did nearly four times Tesla's entire profit problem in revenue. They already share the Terafab chip project (first test chips targeted for May–June 2027), personnel, and a $2 billion Tesla investment in SpaceX stock disclosed in Tesla's Q2 filing. The industrial-overlap argument is genuine.

## The part nobody can committee away

Here is the conflict the structure cannot hide. Musk holds roughly 20% of Tesla, where every share gets one vote — he has complained for years that this is not enough control to build AI and robotics there safely. At SpaceX, super-voting shares give him roughly **82–84% of the vote**. Barclays analyst Dan Levy's math, published October 16 and widely cited since, is that an all-stock acquisition of Tesla by SpaceX would leave Musk with about **37% of the combined company's equity and 56% of its voting power**; other estimates put the voting figure at 56–58%.

In other words: the merger does not just combine two companies. It moves Tesla's shareholders from a structure where Musk can be voted down into one where he effectively cannot — and it does so as a side effect of a deal he proposed, negotiated by committees he appointed directors alongside, for a company he runs on both sides. That is exactly what special committees are for, and the process so far is textbook: independent directors, no terms agreed, Musk recused. But process can make a deal *defensible*; it cannot make the control transfer *free*. Tesla shareholders are being asked to pay for it, in governance, and the price has not been named yet.

The hurdles are correspondingly high:

- **Tesla needs approval from at least 50% of all outstanding shares** — not 50% of votes cast. Musk himself called that "quite a high hurdle." Every passive holder who does nothing counts as a no.
- **It would be the largest merger in history**, reviewed everywhere: Washington may be friendlier under the current administration (reports cited by ANSA say Musk wants to close during Trump's second term), but Europe and China are another matter — Beijing already views SpaceX's US government contracts with suspicion.
- **A buried governance flashpoint is already circulating**: reporting this week that Musk personally ordered a slowdown of Starlink service used by Ukraine's military has been folded into the merger coverage as Exhibit A for what concentrated, unreviewable control means in practice. Fair or not, that is the frame opponents will use.

## My take

Strip the personalities out and there are two separate questions being conflated into one vote.

The industrial question — should the West's largest launch, satellite, EV, robotics, and now AI-infrastructure assets sit in one balance sheet, sharing chips, compute, and talent? — is honestly arguable, and SpaceX's $7.56 billion AI quarter made it more arguable this week, not less. Terafab, space-based compute, and Optimus are not PowerPoint synergies; they are shared physical inputs.

The governance question — should that combination be executed in the one structure that permanently solves Musk's personal control problem at Tesla, without a control premium being explicitly paid to the Tesla shareholders giving control up? — is the one the committees actually have to answer. Watch the exchange ratio, not the keynote. If Tesla holders are paid for the control they surrender, this becomes a expensive-but-legible deal. If they are asked to donate it for "synergy," the 50%-of-all-shares hurdle will do exactly what it was designed to do.

Nothing is agreed, no vote is scheduled, and Tesla's annual-meeting deadline of December 6 is the next date that matters: a proposal that misses it likely waits six to twelve months. Until terms exist, the honest description is the one Musk himself gave it — a good idea, being danced around by the only people allowed to decide it, while the man who wants it watches from outside the room he built.
