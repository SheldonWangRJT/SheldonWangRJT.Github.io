*The allegations below are the newspapers' claims in complaints filed November 2, 2026, as reported by Reuters. They are not findings of a court, and the defendants had not immediately responded at the time of reporting.*

## What was filed

A group of newspapers, led by the New York Times and including the New York Daily News, sued Brave Software in Manhattan federal court on Monday, November 2, 2026. The cases are *New York Times Co v. Brave Software Inc*, No. 1:26-cv-09549, and *Daily News LP v. Brave Software Inc*, No. 1:26-cv-09554, both in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York.

The complaints make three linked accusations: Brave **scrapes** the newspapers' articles for AI training; it **sells** that content to AI companies — naming Anthropic, Perplexity, and xAI, none of which are defendants in these cases; and its browser **lets users circumvent paywalls** to read articles for free. The Times' own summary line, quoted in the complaint, is memorable: Brave "acts both like a burglar that misappropriates the content of publishers, and an AI 'fence' that sells stolen content to third parties." A Times spokesperson, Graham James, said Brave "has flouted established copyright law and ignored repeated requests by The Times to stop its infringing conduct."

## Why Brave, why now

Brave is a peculiar defendant, and that is the point. It is not a frontier model lab like OpenAI — the Times already sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023 in the landmark case still working its way through court. Brave is smaller: a privacy-branded browser and search company that sits between readers, publishers, and AI firms. Suing the middleman tests a different legal theory. If scraping for AI training is infringement, then reselling scraped content to model builders is a second, distinct infringement — and a browser that routes readers around a paywall is a third, aimed at the subscription business itself rather than the training pipeline.

The timing is also pointed. Just two days earlier, on October 31, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, and Anthropic pledged a single standardized way for publishers to block AI training scraping — a voluntary measure that will not fully take effect for months (Microsoft from February 1, 2027, Google sometime in 2027). The Times' lawsuit lands as the industry's voluntary answer is still on paper, and it says, in effect: pledges are not enough, and the middlemen who profit in the meantime need to be sued, not consulted.

There is also history that makes this fight messier than a simple publisher-versus-tech story. Brave had previously sued News Corp preemptively, seeking a declaration that its practices were legal after receiving a cease-and-desist letter; News Corp countersued in July 2026, accusing Brave of "flagrant theft" of Wall Street Journal and New York Post articles and seeking up to $150,000 per infringement. The November 2 suits extend that same war to a second publishing camp. Brave is now fighting publishers on two fronts at once, with opposite procedural postures — plaintiff in one case, defendant in the other.

## The conflict, stated plainly

The publishers' argument is economic before it is legal: every article Brave copies and resells is an article an AI company does not need to license from the publisher, and every paywall bypass is a subscription the publisher does not sell. The more Brave's pipeline works, the less reason anyone has to pay the people who produced the content. That is the same argument the Times has made against OpenAI, extended one step down the supply chain.

Brave's likely defense — it has not yet responded in detail — will lean on the arguments that have protected search engines and browsers for decades: that indexing and linking to public content is fair use, that users, not Brave, choose what to read, and that its search and browser products are transformative tools rather than substitutes for the newspapers themselves. The weakness in that defense, if the complaints' facts hold up, is the resale allegation: a search engine that shows snippets is one thing; a company that sells full article content to AI labs for training is much harder to describe as a mere index.

## The judgment

This case matters less for its damages — unquantified so far — than for where it draws the line. If the Times wins against a middleman, the AI content pipeline gets a toll booth at every stage: labs must license, and so must the brokers who feed them. If Brave wins, the pipeline keeps its cheapest route: scrape once, sell many times, and let the browser do the paywall work for free. Either way, the voluntary opt-out pledge of October 31 looks thinner by the day — the industry's future is being decided in court filings, not in blog posts.
