## A 1933 book became a styling algorithm

The format is always the same, and it is perfect for short video: open a small, beautiful book to a page of color blocks, read out the palette, then cut to an outfit built from it — assembled, almost always, from clothes the creator already owns.

The book is Sanzo Wada's *A Dictionary of Color Combinations* （配色事典）, first compiled in 1933–34 and reissued by Seigensha in 2010. As *Vanity Fair Italia* reported and fashion writers have since repeated, the hashtag **#dictionaryofcolorcombinations** has racked up millions of views on TikTok, with creators such as Lauren Sundstrom, Yolande, and Alessandra Benedetto reinterpreting its palettes as outfits. On Instagram the same format runs as a ritual: creators film a "Day 1", "Day 2", "Day 3" of dressing by the book, one numbered combination at a time.

Why does it work so well on camera? Because the book removes the hardest part of getting dressed — choosing colors — and replaces taste-anxiety with a constraint. You don't need a stylist's eye. You need page 292.

But "inspired by" is doing a lot of work in most trend coverage. So I did the unfashionable thing and checked: which of the popular looks actually follow a specific numbered combination in the book, color for color? Three widely shared ones check out exactly. Here they are, with the book's palette beside the outfit.

## Verified, color for color

### Katie Harrell × Combination No. 292

Katie Harrell (@thekatieharrell, ~18K followers) runs a series building business-casual outfits from the book. For this one she says the quiet part out loud in her caption: *"Pale Lemon Yellow, Ecru, Pinkish Cinnamon, and Isabella Color from the Japanese Dictionary of Color Combinations. Palette 292."*

Eugenia Tobal (@eugeniatobal, ~1.9M followers) is an Argentine actress who launched her own "DÍA 1" of dressing by the book in July 2026 — the post drew ~20.7K likes and 663 comments, most of them asking where to buy the book (she notes, ruefully, that it isn't sold in Argentina). Her caption lists the day's palette: *Pansy Purple, Natural Gray, Hermosa Pink, Sudan Brown*.

On a person, it reads exactly like that.

### Luz Aldán × Combination No. 68

Mexico City creator Luz Aldán (@luzaldan, ~104K followers) started her own "Día 1" in September 2026 with the simplest format in the book — a two-color combination. ## The wider wave

The verified three are the strict end of a much bigger wave:

**Kendall Griffin** (@_kendallgriffin, ~31K followers) posted the most-liked take I've found — ~35.7K likes for an October 2026 fall color-combination guide, walking toward camera in a deep green blazer over a salmon-orange shirt with dark wide-leg trousers, the little book in hand. Green-over-orange is the kind of complementary pairing people are afraid to wear until a 1933 book gives them permission.

## Why a 93-year-old book wins the feed

Three reasons, I think:

1. **It's an anti-haul format.** Almost every look is built from the creator's existing closet. In a genre built on buying, "open a book, wear what you own" is a genuinely different pleasure — and a cheap one to film.
2. **The constraint is the content.** "What do I wear today?" is boring. "Can I dress exactly like page 292?" is a quest with a win condition, and it serializes forever: 348 pages, 348 episodes.
3. **Wada's colors flatter everyone.** His muted, nature-derived palettes — the thing that made them safe for 1930s kimono and print — also happen to be forgiving on every skin tone and kind to phone cameras. Saturated trend colors spike and date; his terracottas and slates just keep looking calm.

## Try it yourself

Pick a combination from the [reference post](/life/wada-dictionary-of-color-combinations/) (No. 273 and No. 292 above are both in it), lay out the closest garments you own in those colors, and assign jobs: biggest garment gets the dominant color, shoes and bag take the accents. "Close enough" is the community's whole philosophy — the palette is a spine, not a dye recipe. If you film it, you're now part of a series that started in Tokyo in 1933. Wada would, I suspect, have found that completely normal: he published the book so people would use it.
