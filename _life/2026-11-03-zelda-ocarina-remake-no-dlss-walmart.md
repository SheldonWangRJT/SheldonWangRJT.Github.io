*This is a launch-week update following our earlier coverage of the Zelda 40th Anniversary Switch 2 console sellout and Walmart''s preorder cancellations. The DLSS finding comes from the game''s license notices as shown in a player-posted clip, not from Nintendo; Walmart''s gift-card offer is reported, not confirmed by Walmart. Prices are US retail as reported November 3, 2026 — a snapshot, subject to change.*

## Finding one: no DLSS in the license screen

On **November 2**, Notebookcheck reported what a player clip showed in the remake''s own software menu: scrolling the version 1.0.0 third-party license notices surfaces **AMD FidelityFX Super Resolution 1.0 (FSR)**, copyrighted 2021, plus **SMAA** anti-aliasing — and no entry mentioning **DLSS**, NVIDIA''s AI upscaler. The remake launches **November 5, exclusively on Switch 2, at $59.99 digital or $69.99 physical** in the US, unlocking at midnight local time.

The caveat matters as much as the finding. A license notice proves code ships in the build; it does not prove when the upscaler runs, at what internal resolution the game renders, or whether DLSS appears elsewhere, and proprietary tools do not always require a notice. The missing entry is a strong signal, not proof. Nintendo has not commented.

But the signal lands because it fits a pattern, not because it is one data point. Donkey Kong Bananza runs FSR 1.0 and SMAA at roughly 1080p–1200p docked, and Mario Kart World shipped without DLSS. Nintendo''s own Switch 2 games, so far, have not used NVIDIA''s flagship upscaler — on a console whose chip is NVIDIA hardware. The technical stakes are narrow and specific: FSR 1.0 is a spatial upscaler, enlarging each frame on its own without earlier frames, cheap on a handheld but recovering less fine detail. Thin geometry — grass, leaves — is where it shows seams, and Notebookcheck notes players on the Switch 2 subreddit already claim to see shimmering foliage in trailers, though trailer compression makes that a weak read. The retail build on November 5 is the verdict.

## Finding two: Walmart''s $519.99 gift card

On **November 3**, GamingBible, citing Polygon and a post from the X account Austin John Plays, reported that Walmart is offering some customers whose limited-edition Zelda Switch 2 preorders it cancelled a **$519.99 Walmart eGift Card**, arriving within 10 business days. Other affected customers reportedly received instead an exclusive reservation to buy the console before general stock, with 48 hours to act. Not every cancelled customer appears to be getting the gift card, and Walmart has not publicly confirmed the offer''s terms.

The number is the story. **$519.99 is exactly the price of the Zelda 40th Anniversary Edition Switch 2 console** — the console that sold out within minutes of going on sale, that Walmart oversold and cancelled preorders for just a day before its October 29 release, and that has since resold for about $600–$800, with scalper listings reported above $1,200 at peak. A gift card for the full console price, to customers Walmart never charged (Walmart generally charges preorders at shipping, not at order), is not a refund. It is an apology priced to end the conversation.

## The judgment

These are two unrelated failures that rhyme. Nintendo''s is a silence problem: a flagship remake of one of gaming''s most beloved titles, three days from launch, with its upscaling approach discovered by players reading license screens rather than announced by the company — and with the discovery feeding an existing fan complaint that the remake uses five-year-old upscaling technology on new hardware. The technical choice itself is defensible: FSR 1.0 is cheap, proven on this hardware, and Nintendo''s first-party precedent. What is not defensible is letting the most engaged fans learn it from a license scroll days before launch, then saying nothing.

Walmart''s is the opposite failure, overcorrected: oversell a limited console, cancel preorders a day before release, then hand some victims a gift card equal to the whole console price while others get a 48-hour reservation window. Generous to the recipients, incoherent as a policy — and a reminder that the real cost of a botched limited-edition launch is not the hardware margin but the trust of the customers who will preorder the next one.

Neither story changes whether the remake is good; that verdict arrives November 5 at midnight, in the retail build, in players'' hands. But launch week has already told us how both companies handle bad news: one says nothing, the other pays full price to make it go away. Buy the game on its reviews, not on either company''s launch-week performance — and if you are a cancelled Walmart customer still waiting on an offer, check your email before you pay a reseller anything.
