This is the seventh entry in the 🔥 Incident Recap series.

Start with the sentence that should make every infrastructure engineer pause: **the official investigation does not know why this fire started — and it did not need to, to explain why a building was lost.** On the night of March 9–10, 2021, at OVHcloud's Strasbourg campus on the Port du Rhin, an electrical fault occurred almost simultaneously at an uninterruptible power supply unit and at the lead-acid batteries connected to it, in two separate ground-floor energy rooms of the SBG2 data center. By 10:02 the next morning, when firefighters finally extinguished it, SBG2 was totally destroyed, four of the twelve rooms of neighboring SBG1 were gone, and — according to the public sources cited in the investigation — roughly **3.6 million websites across 464,000 domain names** had been unavailable at the height of the crisis, nearly 18% of the active IP addresses assigned to OVH. Nobody was killed or injured. That fact is what makes it possible to study this incident as an engineering object rather than a tragedy.

Every previous entry in this series was a software incident wearing an operations costume. Meta's BGP outage (entry #1) was configuration. Cloudflare's regex outage (entry #2) was code. Fastly (entry #3) was a latent bug, AWS us-east-1 (entry #4) a feedback loop, Knight Capital (entry #5) a deployment, GitHub (entry #6) an automation doing exactly what it was told. This one is different in kind: the failing layer is physical — air, water, electricity, and the building itself — and the "cascade" is not requests amplifying through a system but fire propagating through a structure that had been optimized, deliberately and for good reasons, to let air move through it freely. The mechanisms below are therefore not about code paths. They are about what happens when the assumptions underneath your redundancy — that a second building is an independent failure domain, that a backup is separate from the thing it backs up, that you can always turn the power off — turn out to be descriptions of a campus that shares one fate.

A note on sources, because this incident demands one. OVHcloud never published a postmortem of the kind GitHub or Cloudflare write. The primary reconstruction is the investigation report of France's **BEA-RI** (Bureau d'enquêtes et d'analyses sur les risques industriels), report MTE-BEARI-2022-005, dated May 24, 2022 — a safety investigation whose stated purpose is prevention, not blame, and which explicitly declines to determine the precise cause of the fire because the judicial expert assessment was still ongoing. The second primary source is OVHcloud's own running incident page, "Fire at our Strasbourg Site," updated from March 10 to April 2, 2021. Where this article uses trade-press reporting of OVHcloud's later statements (the free-backup pledge and the "hyper resilience" plan), it says so. And where the BEA-RI says a thing is unknown, this article says it is unknown. On this incident, inventing a root cause would be the easiest way to write a confident, wrong article.

## Background: five buildings, one campus, and a tower designed for airflow

OVHcloud, founded in 1999, told investigators it served more than 1.5 million customers from 32 data centers on four continents. The Strasbourg site, in the Port du Rhin industrial zone near the German border, consisted of five buildings built in successive expansion phases — and the fact that they were built at different times, to different designs, turns out to matter enormously. SBG1, the first phase (commissioned in 2012), was built by stacking shipping-container-style modules three levels high. SBG2 kept the modular idea but rose to six levels on a steel structure, with prefabricated concrete caissons, floors of raw wood treated with intumescent coating, and single-skin or aluminum cladding — a design whose stated objective was to promote heat exchange with the outside and cut the energy spent cooling the equipment inside. SBG3 was more conventional concrete construction. SBG4, in operation since 2013, was a single-level building comparable to SBG1. SBG5 was still under construction and not in operation.

Power followed the standard data-center pattern, and its details drive the whole night. The site was fed by two redundant 20 kV links; diesel generators could carry the site if the grid failed; and "energy rooms" on the ground floor held large quantities of lead-acid batteries — SBG2 alone housed 800 batteries of 30 kg each — feeding the UPS inverters that bridge the gap between a grid failure and the generators taking over. The batteries were not equipped with a scanning or supervision system; OVH told investigators it respected the manufacturer's recommended lifespans. Fire protection, across the whole site, consisted of a detection system (optical and aspirating smoke detectors) plus permanently present staff trained with extinguishers. **No building on the Strasbourg site had an automatic extinguishing system.** The site's fire water came from the public network: a single supply line and one fire hydrant.

Two properties of this setup are the incident in embryo. First, SBG2's celebrated efficiency was an airflow property: the building was, in the BEA-RI's words, designed to promote the circulation of outside air through facade openings. A building that breathes well for cooling also breathes well for smoke and flame — the same openings, the same vertical paths. Second, everything on the campus was coupled through electricity. Making the site safe for firefighters meant neutralizing not one power source but four layers of it — grid, generators that start automatically, UPS inverters, and charged batteries — some of which were specifically designed to resist being turned off. Keep both properties in mind; the timeline is what they produce.

Read the diagram by its couplings, not its boxes. There are four of them, and each one is a place where a boundary that looked real on an architecture diagram turned out not to exist in the physical world. The sections below take them in order.

## Mechanism 1: a building optimized to move air cannot compartment a fire

The first coupling is between energy efficiency and fire spread, and it is the most literal mechanism in this series: the same physical property was both the feature and the failure.

SBG2 was designed to exchange heat with the outside. Facade openings promoted the circulation of outside air; the internal structure and floors carried one-hour fire treatments (intumescent paint, flocking), but the floors were raw wood and the cladding was single-skin — choices the BEA-RI lists, along with the absence of floor overhangs on the facade, as not slowing the fire's progression enough given the time needed to bring serious water to bear. The detector chronology is the smoking gun, literally: within 15 minutes of the first alarm, aspirating smoke detectors had triggered on the ground floor **and on every upper floor**, some of them only minutes after ignition. Smoke is fire's advance party — it carries heat and unburned fuel ahead of the flame front — and in SBG2 it had an unobstructed vertical highway. By 01:28 the first floor was fully involved; by about 01:40 temperature sensors two floors up were saturating; by about 02:00 firefighters reported the generalized conflagration of the whole building.

The causal chain is worth stating in the form this series demands. Free cooling requires permeability: air must enter low, pass the equipment, and leave high, with as little obstruction as the design can manage. Fire compartmentation requires the opposite: sealed floors, fire-stopped penetrations, doors that close, and a facade that does not offer the fire a fresh supply of oxygen or a path around each floor slab. You can engineer a building that does both — with dampers, rated shafts, and suppression doing the work that openness refuses to — but each reconciliation costs money and efficiency, and SBG2's design, built to the standards of its day as OVH's founder later noted, had chosen the efficiency end of the tradeoff. The BEA-RI's phrasing is careful and devastating: the building showed "great permeability to the outside air and therefore to fire smoke, which can spread quickly and without difficulty."

**What would not have fixed this.** A better alarm would have changed nothing — detection worked perfectly, and this article returns to that uncomfortable fact below. More fire resistance in the battery room alone would also not have saved the building, a point the BEA-RI makes explicitly and builds a regulatory recommendation on: the fire started in **two** rooms almost simultaneously, at the batteries and at the inverter connected to them, and the inverter room was not the room the battery-charging regulations targeted. Harden one origin and the other still burns. That is a general lesson about single-point hardening in systems with coupled origins, and it applies to software exactly as it applies to buildings.

## Mechanism 2: detection without suppression is a notification, not a control

The second coupling is between knowing and acting. Strasbourg's detection did everything asked of it. The aspirating and optical systems, in the BEA-RI's words, "played their role of detection perfectly": the alarm reached the guard post at 00:35, the guard confirmed thick black smoke at 00:37, the building was evacuated by 00:39, and the fire service was called at 00:42. Nobody was hurt, and the report credits detection and the on-site staff for that. As a life-safety system, it succeeded completely.

As a property-protection system, it was a sensor wired to nothing. None of the five Strasbourg buildings had an automatic extinguishing system — a choice, the report notes, that the applicable regulations did not forbid, since most of a data-center building falls outside the classified-installation rules that would have imposed one, and the industry's own 2020 white paper treated automatic suppression as recommended rather than required. So the sequence after a perfect detection was: humans confirm, humans call, firefighters travel, firefighters assess — and firefighters, arriving at 00:59 to find electric arcs in the energy room, correctly refuse to put serious water on an electrically live building. The first control that could actually act on the fire at scale was gated behind the electrical problem of Mechanism 3. An automatic system, the BEA-RI points out, could have applied water (or gas, or mist) "very early in the accident sequence, without even waiting for the interruption of the electricity supply, and without exposing personnel to the risk of electrocution." That sentence is the mechanism: suppression is the only layer that acts inside the window when a fire is still small, and Strasbourg had deliberately left that layer empty, relying on a chain of human and municipal steps whose total latency — about two hours to full water — exceeded the building's time-to-flashover by an order of magnitude.

**The boundary of this mechanism.** Suppression is not magic, and the BEA-RI does not claim it would certainly have saved SBG2: the fire's two simultaneous origins, one of them in a battery room full of stored chemical energy, are close to a worst case for any fixed system, and the report's recommendation is about the system as a whole — detection, protection, construction, and a rehearsed intervention plan — not about a single device. The transferable claim is narrower and harder to argue with: if your mitigation for a fast failure mode is a chain of humans, phone calls, and another organization's trucks, you do not have a mitigation for that failure mode. You have a notification that it is happening. Every software organization has an equivalent: the alert that pages a human to manually fail over a system whose failure completes in ninety seconds.

## Mechanism 3: the redundancy that fights the shutdown — 95 minutes to turn the power off

The third coupling is the most counterintuitive, and the BEA-RI states it as a general principle: in a data center, "power supply redundancies and electrical backup groups, which in normal times are essential to continuity of service, may hinder the intervention of the public emergency services." Strasbourg is the worked example.

Follow the timestamps. The order to cut power was given at 00:58 — twenty-three minutes after the alarm, which is fast. OVH cut SBG2's emergency power at 01:13 and the other buildings' at 01:28. The grid operator, notified at 00:52, could not intervene at the site's secondary substation (it belonged to the client, and by the time its team arrived at 01:27 the fire made intervention there impossible), so it cut the supply remotely at the upstream source substation at 01:50. And at 02:14 — an hour and a quarter after the cut order — **there was still current in SBG2**. Investigators estimate the site was electrically safe only around 02:30, and current was fully absent only from 03:28. Why does a building with its grid feed cut stay live for over an hour? Because it was designed to. The diesel generators start automatically when the grid drops — that is their entire purpose — so OVH teams had to neutralize them by hand, in a burning building's campus, during the fire. Behind the generators stand the batteries: 800 of them in SBG2, charged, and estimated to keep supplying power for roughly twenty minutes after everything upstream of them is cut. There was, the report notes, no general site cut-off device easily accessible in an emergency; the substations sat near the intervention area; and there was no pre-established, rehearsed procedure for making this specific site electrically safe — no listed-establishment plan with the fire service at all.

The mechanism, stated as a causal chain: each layer of power redundancy is an independent energy source with its own shutdown procedure, its own owner, and in the generators' case an automatic restart behavior that actively opposes the shutdown. The firefighters' constraint is absolute — significant water waits for electrical safety — so the time to neutralize every layer is added directly to the fire's free-burning time. Strasbourg's layers summed to roughly 95 minutes from cut order to a safe site, during which the fire went from two ground-floor rooms to a generalized conflagration that also took four rooms of the next building. Redundancy designed against one failure (loss of grid) became an accelerant of another (fire) because nobody had designed the **combined** operation: all sources off, verifiably, fast, by a procedure a fire crew can execute at 1 a.m.

**What this mechanism does not excuse, and what it cannot fix.** None of this makes the delay anyone's improvisation failure; the BEA-RI treats it as a design and preparedness gap, which is why its recommendations to OVH are about pre-established emergency procedures, exercised with the fire service, and about auditing every site for fire vulnerability. And the honest limit runs the other way too: even a perfect 10-minute cutoff would have left firefighters facing a permeable six-story tower with no suppression and one hydrant. The mechanisms in this incident are multiplicative, not alternative explanations. Fixing any one of them improves the outcome; the building burned because all of them pointed the same way on the same night.

## Mechanism 4: water — the resource nobody had sized

The fourth coupling is embarrassingly physical. The public fire network available to the responders consisted of a single hydrant delivering less than 60 m³/h. OVH had no extinguishing water reserve of its own and no means of pumping from the Rhine canal the site sits beside. At 01:49 the EUROPA pump boat — a Franco-German vessel with three pumps of 15,000 liters per minute — was requested; it arrived at 02:57 and, in the report's judgment, played a determining role in containing the fire and protecting SBG1 and SBG3; without it, the consequences for the adjacent buildings would probably have been greater. About 4,000 liters of foam concentrate were used before the fire was extinguished at 10:02.

The lesson here is not "buy a boat." It is that a resource can be simultaneously adjacent, abundant, and unavailable: the site sits on a river port, surrounded by water it had no equipment to use, dependent on a municipal hydrant sized for an ordinary industrial building rather than for a six-story energy-dense tower. Capacity planning had been done, rigorously, for the resources the business runs on — power, cooling, bandwidth, servers (OVH would triple its server assembly lines within days). It had not been done for the resource the worst night runs on. Every system has an EUROPA-shaped dependency somewhere: the thing you will desperately need during the incident that no dashboard tracks because it is never used on a normal day.

## Mechanism 5: the campus was one failure domain — and so were the backups

The fire destroyed one building. The outage took four. SBG3 and SBG4 were, in OVH's own status language, "spared from the fire" and their servers undamaged — and they were dark anyway, because the campus-wide electrical isolation that firefighting required cut their power at 01:28, and because restarting them meant diagnostics, cleaning soot and water, and a room-by-room, aisle-by-aisle, rack-by-rack restart under safety protocol, at about seven hours of cleaning per rack. SBG4 reached 100% of its 80 racks only toward the end of March; SBG3's first racks returned on March 19. SBG1, the building that actually burned alongside SBG2, was never reactivated in place — a decision sealed on March 19, when smoke appeared in an **unconnected battery room** there (batteries, disconnected, can still smoke; stored energy does not care about your topology diagrams) and OVH cut power to SBG1 and SBG4 again on the precautionary principle. Its surviving servers were cleaned, shipped to Croix for inspection and repair, and reinstalled in other data centers.

And then there is the layer that turned an outage into data loss. A backup that lives in the same failure domain as the primary is not a backup against that domain's failure — it is a second copy scheduled to die in the same event. OVHcloud's own later statements, as reported in the trade press, conceded the structure: internal backups had been kept within a data center or, in some cases, another data center **in the same region**, and customers discovered in March 2021 that some services' backups had burned or gone dark with the servers they protected. The company's response is the tell that this was the deepest lesson it took: founder Octave Klaba announced on March 17 that backups would become free and provided by default for all customers, saying the incident would change the industry's standard; and in May, OVHcloud laid out a five-year "hyper resilience" plan whose first plank was a new region model with internal backups physically separated from the operational regions, alongside three-data-center failover per region. By mid-April it reported 118,000 of the 120,000 affected services restored — a recovery measured in weeks of logistics: 2,100 replacement servers delivered on the day of the fire itself, production tripled to 3,000–4,000 servers a week, about 15,000 new servers committed.

**The boundary.** Geographic separation is not free, and Strasbourg does not prove that every backup must be on another continent. It proves a narrower thing: the separation distance of a backup must exceed the blast radius of the failure it exists to survive, and the blast radius must be computed from the physical and operational couplings — shared power isolation, shared campus, shared region — not from the logical diagram where two boxes in different buildings look independent. SBG3's servers survived the fire and were useless for days; SBG1's batteries smoked nine days later. "Different building, same campus" was inside the blast radius both times.

## What the investigation does not say

This article's honesty ledger is unusually important, because the most repeated "fact" about this fire in popular retellings — that a UPS serviced that morning caused it — is not an established fact. Here is what the BEA-RI actually found. The fire broke out almost simultaneously, around 00:35, in two energy rooms, at UPS2 (the report's ASI2) and at the batteries connected to it, which were in a different room. The inverter had been maintained in the preceding days for recurring unexplained switches to bypass mode; the maintenance and recommissioning reports mention nothing singular. Sensors recorded anomalous humidity around 23:15 and again after 00:30; the investigators could not establish whether those readings were measurement error or a real presence of liquid, and they note only that liquid or moisture inside an electrical device *can* form an internal short circuit of the kind that could produce the observed damage. OVH could not provide the inverters' operating parameters at the moment of failure. The report's conclusion is explicit: "It is not possible, at this stage, to establish the cause of the failure at the level of the UPS," and it lists the live hypotheses — liquid or humidity linked to the nearby cooling system, a malfunction linked to the maintenance operation, operation of the inverter outside its normal ranges — without choosing among them. The judicial experts' conclusions were not available to the BEA-RI and are not in this article. Anyone who tells you the cause of the Strasbourg fire with confidence is telling you more than the official investigation knows.

Two smaller gaps deserve the same honesty. The 3.6 million websites figure comes, as the BEA-RI itself attributes it, from public sources (Netcraft's count, as widely reported) rather than from OVH's own accounting, and the total financial damage was left to the Strasbourg court's expert panel, whose conclusions were not known at the report's date. And OVHcloud's incident page, this article's second primary source, is a customer-communications document: it is detailed and timestamped about recovery logistics, and silent about the fire's cause and about which specific services lost data permanently. The article has used it for what it is good for — the recovery timeline — and not for what it does not contain.

## Lessons worth stealing

1. **Your failure domains are physical before they are logical.** Four buildings, one campus power isolation, one outage. Draw your blast-radius map from the couplings that actually transmit failure — shared power, shared cooling, shared network rooms, shared geography — not from the boxes on the architecture diagram. If two "independent" systems must both be powered off to save either of them, they are one system.
2. **Efficiency mechanisms are propagation mechanisms with the sign flipped.** The airflow that made SBG2 cheap to cool carried smoke to every floor in 15 minutes. Every optimization that removes resistance from a path — caching, connection pooling, shared queues, permeable buildings — also removes resistance from whatever failure travels that path. When you adopt an optimization, name the failure it accelerates.
3. **Detection without an actuator is telemetry, not mitigation.** Strasbourg's detection was perfect and the building burned down on schedule. Audit your own fast failure modes the same way: for each one, is there a mechanism that acts, automatically, inside the failure's own timescale — or only a page, a call tree, and another team's trucks?
4. **Design the all-off path with the same care as the never-off path.** Redundancy layers that auto-restart will fight an emergency shutdown. OVH's generators, inverters, and batteries each had to be neutralized separately, by different actors, without a rehearsed site procedure, costing roughly 95 minutes of free-burning time. If your system has an emergency stop, it needs one procedure, one owner, and a drill — tested against the auto-restart behaviors you built on purpose.
5. **Size the resources the worst day consumes.** One hydrant under 60 m³/h, no reserve, no pump — beside a river. The resources an incident consumes (water, spare servers, cleaning crews, a founder's Twitter account as the status page) are never the ones the capacity plan tracks. List yours before the incident does.
6. **A backup inside the blast radius is a second primary.** OVHcloud's own corrective plan — backups physically separated into a different region, free and by default — is the industry-standard conclusion stated by the company that paid for the lesson. Verify where every copy physically lives, and compute the distance against the largest failure you claim to survive, not the average one.
7. **Harden coupled origins together, or accept that single-point hardening is decoration.** Fire-rated battery rooms alone would have contained one of Strasbourg's two simultaneous origins. The BEA-RI's regulatory recommendation — treat the battery room and the room holding the equipment that charges and converts for those batteries as one "charging workshop" — generalizes: a guardrail that covers only the most obvious member of a coupled set covers neither.
8. **Disconnected does not mean inert.** Nine days after the fire, an unconnected battery room in SBG1 smoked and forced a second precautionary campus power cut. Stored energy — chemical, and by analogy queued work, retained data, standing credentials — persists after you "turn a system off." Decommissioning plans that ignore stored energy are plans for a second incident.
